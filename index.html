Healthcare Machine Learning

Stroke Risk Prediction

Connecting exploratory health analysis, patient segmentation, and symptom-based prediction in one reproducible notebook.

TimelineFall 2025
SkillsPython, scikit-learn, XGBoost, K-Means, PCA, GMM

Project Overview & Description

A machine-learning project analyzing a health dataset with 14,976 rows and 19 original columns, including risk measures and the target label. The notebook combines engineered risk indices, clustering, regression of stroke-risk percentage, and classification of at-risk status.

Objective

Compare predictive models using held-out evaluation, identify patterns associated with the supplied risk measures, and make patient-group differences easier to interpret.

Approach

  • Explored symptom distributions and engineered age-normalized risk, chronic-condition, symptom-burden, and age-adjusted risk indices.
  • Standardized five features for K-Means, explored cluster counts using elbow and silhouette analysis, and compared clustering with Gaussian mixture models. The saved profiling run uses four clusters.
  • Visualized the clusters with two-component PCA, which retained 81.1% of variance in the standardized clustering features.
  • Compared five classifiers and five regressors using 15 symptom indicators and an 80/20 train–test split (random state 42). A separate Logistic Regression submission workflow used age, gender, and symptoms.

Results & Key Findings

  • MLP Classifier: ROC AUC 0.8854, accuracy 80.14%, and F1 0.8362 on the notebook’s held-out split.
  • Logistic Regression: ROC AUC 0.8853, accuracy 80.34%, and F1 0.8310. These are a separate model’s results, not a combined best-model score.
  • The four interpreted clusters had mean supplied risk percentages of 35.23%, 56.36%, 85.89%, and 94.59%.
  • The saved MLP regression result was RMSE 18.160 and R² 0.532; the final submission file contained 3,745 predictions.

Impact & Conclusion

This project demonstrates the complementary roles of interpretable exploration and predictive comparison. Cluster labels describe the supplied risk data rather than independently validated clinical categories. The recorded metrics come from a single dataset and held-out split; they do not establish clinical validity or real-world treatment utility.

Quantitative Finance

Portfolio Optimization

From stock screening and one-step price forecasts to a transparent comparison of mean–variance, Black–Litterman, and equal-weight allocations.

TimelineSpring 2024
SkillsR, Python, LSTM, Random Forest, XGBoost, Mean–Variance, Black–Litterman

Project Overview & Description

Originally developed in Spring 2024, this project starts with 30 Taiwan stocks and 10 ETF benchmarks. It preserves the presentation’s 22-stock screened set and final six-stock selection, while the maintained repository separates historical portfolio reconstruction from newly trained forecasting models and in-sample allocation refits.

Objective

Investigate how correlation screening, price forecasting, and allocation choices affect risk and return, and compare the historical stock portfolios with ETF alternatives under explicit calculation assumptions.

Approach

  • Aligned local closing-price workbooks and examined returns, correlations, cross-correlations, and recurrence plots. Stock screening uses an absolute log-return correlation threshold of 0.6.
  • Compared Random Forest, XGBoost, and LSTM one-step forecasts across the 22-stock set, using 20 observed prices per input window and chronological 80/20 train–test splits.
  • Reconstructed the presentation’s mean–variance, Black–Litterman, and equal-weight six-stock portfolios using normalized initial capital weights and fixed initial share holdings.
  • Evaluated January 3–November 30, 2023 price performance against 10 ETFs, using 252 trading days for annualization and a 1.565% annual risk-free rate. Newly refitted allocations are reported separately.

Results & Key Findings

  • Historical mean–variance portfolio: 40.40% period price return, 15.10% annualized volatility, and Sharpe ratio 2.584.
  • Historical Black–Litterman portfolio: 38.71% period price return, 17.82% annualized volatility, and Sharpe ratio 2.134.
  • Historical equal-weight portfolio: 39.57% period price return, 16.57% annualized volatility, and Sharpe ratio 2.326.
  • Each portfolio’s period price return exceeded 7 of the 10 ETF benchmarks. ETF 00757 returned more, while 00713 had lower volatility and a higher Sharpe ratio.

Impact & Conclusion

The project makes the trade-offs between return, risk, and allocation assumptions inspectable through code and saved tables. These are retrospective price-return comparisons, not evidence of an out-of-sample investment strategy: distributions, transaction costs, taxes, and corporate-action adjustments are excluded, and refitted portfolios use the same window for fitting and evaluation. Historical selection and current model retraining should not be treated as one prospective backtest.

Deep Learning · Sequence Modeling

Transformer Arithmetic Reasoning

A small Transformer that learns to generate the intermediate steps of a controlled arithmetic task, one token at a time.

TimelineFall 2025
SkillsPython, PyTorch, Causal Attention, Autoregressive Modeling, Curriculum Learning

Project Overview & Description

A deep-learning project that trains a compact causal Transformer on synthetically generated arithmetic. The workflow combines character-level tokenization, curriculum training, exact-match evaluation, and standalone CPU inference. Inputs are positive-integer addition expressions ending in an equals sign; outputs include every intermediate reduction and the final answer.

Objective

Learn correct full-sequence arithmetic reductions across different operand counts and digit lengths without a pretrained language model, and evaluate the entire generated sequence rather than only the final number.

Approach

  • Encoded expressions with a 17-token character vocabulary and dynamically padded variable-length batches. Padding tokens are excluded from next-token cross-entropy loss.
  • Configured six Transformer layers with six attention heads, a 192-dimensional representation, learned positional embeddings, and an explicit causal attention mask.
  • Trained with AdamW through three curriculum phases totaling 18,000 steps, increasing expression length and operand digit count.
  • Used greedy autoregressive decoding and exact string matching on 1,000 generated examples per benchmark setting; saved the weights and a command-line inference script.

Results & Key Findings

  • The saved notebook reports 100% exact-sequence accuracy in all 10 benchmark settings, with 1,000 examples per setting.
  • The settings cover 2–3 operands with 1–3 digits and 4–5 operands with 1–2 digits.
  • The repository includes trained weights and predict.py for standalone CPU inference, including the recorded reduction (((1+2)+1)+8)=((3+1)+8)=(4+8)=12.

Impact & Conclusion

The work demonstrates causal masking, sequence batching, curriculum training, and the separation of training from inference. Its strong benchmark result is specific to synthetic positive-integer addition under the generator’s rules, with a 128-token context limit. It is not evidence of general mathematical reasoning, and broader out-of-distribution generalization remains untested.

Deep Learning · Computer Vision

Butterfly Species Classification

Turning long-tailed butterfly observations into a practical image-classification workflow, from label engineering to probabilistic inference.

TimelineFall 2025
SkillsPython, PyTorch, CNNs, CUDA AMP, Data Augmentation, Model Evaluation

Project Overview & Description

A computer-vision project using iNaturalist-derived butterfly observations. The nine most frequent training species are modeled separately, while remaining species are grouped as Other, creating 10 target classes. The repository includes the training notebook, tabular metadata, processed labels, and test predictions, but not the full image collection.

Objective

Design and train a CNN from scratch, manage the long-tailed label distribution, and select a checkpoint using validation performance before producing class probabilities for unseen test images.

Approach

  • Explored class frequencies and constructed a shared top-nine-plus-Other label mapping for training and validation.
  • Implemented a custom PyTorch Dataset and DataLoaders, 224 × 224 RGB preprocessing, ImageNet normalization, and training-time augmentation.
  • Built five convolutional stages with batch normalization and ReLU, two max-pooling steps, a 256-to-64 channel bottleneck, and a 10-logit classifier.
  • Trained with Adam, cross-entropy loss, CUDA mixed precision, gradient clipping at 5.0, and early stopping after three epochs without validation-loss improvement.

Results & Key Findings

  • The selected checkpoint at epoch 25 achieved 79.22% validation accuracy and 0.6141 validation loss.
  • At that checkpoint, training accuracy was 78.44% and training loss was 0.6391.
  • The inference workflow exports a probability for each of the 10 classes per test image. Test accuracy is not reported because test ground-truth labels are unavailable.

Impact & Conclusion

This project connects data preparation, model architecture, GPU training, checkpoint selection, and inference in an end-to-end workflow. Grouping rare species into Other simplifies the task but limits fine-grained identification; class-specific evaluation and comparison with pretrained backbones are useful next steps, not completed results.

Side Project · Scheduling

Mahjong Tournament Scheduler

A practical scheduling tool built for organizing Mahjong tournaments and playtesting with friends.

TimelineSummer 2026
SkillsPython, Tkinter, Heuristic Scheduling, Input Validation, pytest, CSV / JSON

Project Overview & Description

A Python application that turns a player list and required appearances into a validated sequence of four-player matches. It exposes a command-line interface, a Tkinter desktop interface, and a reusable Python API, using only the standard library at runtime.

Objective

Give every player the requested number of appearances, keep each match to four distinct participants, and reduce repeated pairings and consecutive appearances while making the schedule easy to review and export.

Approach

  • Validated player names, equal appearance requirements, and divisibility of total player seats by four before generation.
  • Built candidate schedules with randomized, feasibility-aware selection from eligible four-player groups, penalizing repeated pairs and consecutive appearances.
  • Compared complete schedules using pair-frequency variance, maximum pair frequency, and consecutive appearances; retained the lowest-scoring valid candidate across multiple attempts.
  • Added seeded generation, player-by-player and pairing summaries, TXT / CSV / JSON exports, and tests covering constraints, reproducibility, validation, and export formats.

Results & Key Findings

  • The checked-in sample shows eight players appearing four times each across eight four-player matches, with a VALID validation result.
  • The application provides both desktop and command-line workflows, plus inspectable match lists and per-player appearance summaries.
  • The repository includes automated tests for schedule constraints, repeated-seed consistency, invalid input handling, and all three export formats; no tournament-scale performance benchmark is claimed.

Impact & Conclusion

The tool replaces manual schedule bookkeeping with reproducible generation and explicit validation. It is a randomized heuristic, not a guaranteed optimum: repeated four-player groups may occur, valid requests may exhaust the attempt limit, and large player pools can be slow. Its practical value is transparent scheduling support rather than a claim of perfect fairness.
